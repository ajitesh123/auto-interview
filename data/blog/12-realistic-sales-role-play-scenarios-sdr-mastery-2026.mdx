Start with brush-offs: in Gong's study of more than 300 million cold calls, 49.5% of objections were dismissive ("Not interested", "Send me some information") and the top 5 objections made up 74% of all of them. ## Build a round now

Pick a scenario, give the buyer card to whoever plays the buyer (a manager, a peer or an AI buyer), and give the rep only the rep card. The hidden reason is the point of the drill: the rep wins by finding it, not by reciting a rebuttal.

## Which objections to practise first

**Practise brush-offs first, then money and timing, then existing-solution objections, because that is the order in which reps meet them.** Gong's breakdown of 300M+ cold calls puts half of all objections in the dismissive group. Most of them arrive in the first 30 seconds, before the rep has said anything worth objecting to.

## How to score a round

**Score every round on the same 4 lines, 1, 3 or 5 points each, so a total of 4 to 20 means the same thing every week.** The buyer and one observer score; the rep scores too, and the gap between the rep's score and the others' is often the most useful part of the debrief.

<RoleplayRubricTable />

What the totals mean:

- **16–20:** ready. Move to hard difficulty or a new scenario.
- **10–15:** close. Rerun the same scenario at once and fix only the lowest line.
- **4–9:** not yet. Rerun it the next day on easy, then try again.

Why these 4 lines? "Pause and ask" and "Relevance" come from Gong's call data above. On cold calls Gong also found that opening with "Did I catch you at a bad time?" went with a 40% lower chance of booking a meeting, while stating the reason for calling went with a 2.1x higher success rate ([Gong, 16 Aug 2021](https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-call-stats)); scenario 1's must-do is built on that. "Next step" is there because a friendly call that ends with "I'll send you an email" is still a lost call. ## How to run a session

**Run roleplay as short, repeated rounds of one scenario, not a single long performance in front of the team.**

<RoleplaySessionFlow />

The rules that make it work:

1. **The buyer follows the card, not their mood.** Most of the awkwardness reps complain about comes from a buyer who either caves at once or turns cruel. The warm-up and end rules fix that.
2. **The rep never sees the buyer card.** Knowing the hidden reason turns discovery into a quiz.
3. **Nobody pauses the call to coach.** Notes go on the rubric; the debrief comes after.
4. **Rerun the same scenario straight away.** The second round, fixing one line, is where the learning shows.
5. **Come back to the weakest scenario later in the week.** A meta-analysis of 317 experiments found spaced practice beats massed practice for remembering ([Cepeda et al., 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)); the studies were on verbal recall, but the advice to space reruns is cheap to follow.

## Does roleplay change what reps do on real calls?

**Practice builds the skill reliably; whether it shows up on real calls depends on what managers do afterwards.** A meta-analysis of 117 behaviour-modelling training studies (Taylor, Russ-Eft and Chan, _Journal of Applied Psychology_, 2005) found large effects on knowledge and skills but only modest effects on job behaviour ([ScienceForWork summary](https://scienceforwork.com/blog/training-make-change-stick/); [QIC-WD summary](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Behavior%20Modeling%20Training%20112421b.pdf)). The same summary lists what helped transfer, including showing both good and bad examples, letting learners write their own scenarios and training the learners' managers.

Coaching rhythm matters too. In CSO Insights' 2019 enablement study of more than 900 participants, teams with a "dynamic" coaching approach had an average win rate of 55.2%, 13.4 points higher than teams that coached at random ([CSO Insights, 2019](https://community.highspot.com/assets/2019/10/CSO-Insights-5th-Annual-Sales-Enablement-Study.pdf)). That is a correlation, not proof that coaching caused the gap.

Ramp is the reason managers care. The Bridge Group's 2025 report on 351 B2B companies puts average SDR ramp at 3.0 months and tenure at 1.9 years ([Bridge Group, 6 Feb 2025](https://bridgegroupinc.com/research/sdr-tenure-and-ramp-time)), so every week of ramp a team saves is a real share of an SDR's time in the seat.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are good sales roleplay scenarios?

Scenarios built on objections your buyers really say, with a hidden reason the rep has to uncover, a rule for when the buyer warms up and a single goal for the rep. Start with brush-offs such as "Not interested", "Send me some information" and "Call me in 6 months": Gong found dismissive objections are 49.5% of all cold-call objections.

### How long should a sales roleplay be?

One round of 3 to 6 minutes (2 to 7 depending on difficulty) is enough for a single objection; a full session with a brief, a scored round and an immediate rerun takes about 20 minutes. Short rounds repeated beat one long performance.

### How do you score a sales roleplay?

Use the same few lines every time with defined levels. Ours: pause and ask, relevance, the scenario's must-do, and next step, each 1, 3 or 5 points, for a total of 4 to 20. 16 or more means ready for live calls on that scenario.

### How do you make sales roleplay less awkward?

Give the buyer a card with fixed rules for when to warm up and when to end the call, keep the rep from seeing it, never stop the call to coach, and score on a rubric instead of giving opinions in front of the team. Practising alone with an AI buyer first also helps.

### What are good sales roleplay scenarios for an interview?

Interviewers usually ask for a short cold call or a "sell me this" exercise and then throw one objection. Scenarios 1, 4 and 7 (meeting brush-off, "not interested", "too expensive") are good rehearsals; the rubric shows what interviewers listen for.
